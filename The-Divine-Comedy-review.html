Nine Circles, No Stars

Our inspector books a descent through Hell, a climb up Purgatory, and a stay in Paradise. One destination is unforgettable. The other two need work.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Dante Alighieri, The Divine Comedy◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

Our inspector arrived without a reservation, in the middle of his life, having taken a wrong turn into a dark wood. He was met at the gate by a sign advising visitors to abandon all hope, which is not a welcome we have encountered elsewhere.

A word on the property. Dante Alighieri, a Florentine poet writing in the early 1300s, imagined himself taken on a guided tour of the afterlife: down through Hell, up the mountain of Purgatory, and out into the light of Paradise. His guide for the descent is the Roman poet Virgil, dead thirteen centuries and evidently still working. We assess all three destinations below.

Hell — nine circles, no stars

The layout is the most impressive thing about the place. Hell is a funnel of nine descending circles, and the assignment of guests to floors is done with a precision most hotels can only envy. The rule is that the punishment mirrors the sin: those who let lust blow them through life are blown forever on a black wind; those who told the world what would happen next now walk with their heads twisted backward, seeing only where they have been. The Italians call this contrapasso, and it is the single best piece of design in medieval literature.

Service is attentive if unfriendly. Amenities: none. Climate ranges from boiling pitch to, at the very bottom, a lake of ice — a genuine surprise for guests expecting flames throughout.

Every reader remembers the design of Hell. Almost nobody finishes the tour.

The guide

Virgil is the finest tour guide in literature and works, as far as we can establish, for nothing. He explains the geology, handles the difficult residents, carries his visitor when the path gives out, and never once loses patience with a man who keeps stopping to talk to celebrities. He is also, by the rules of the place, not permitted to leave it, having lived before Christianity; the tour ends for him at the top of the mountain. It is the saddest contract in the book.

A note on the guest list

Prospective visitors should know that Dante wrote this while exiled from Florence by his political enemies, and that a striking number of those enemies turn up in the lower circles, named, suffering, and identified by hometown. Popes are here. Neighbors are here. It is the most elegant act of revenge in the history of European letters, and it is entirely deliberate.

Where the stay disappoints

Honesty compels us. The first third is magnificent; the rest is a long climb. Purgatory is thoughtful but slower, and Paradise — where Dante ascends through the spheres of heaven discussing theology — loses most visitors entirely. The higher he goes, the more abstract it gets, and light is much harder to describe than fire.

There is also the footnote problem. Dante wrote for readers who knew exactly which Florentine banker he meant, and modern editions carry more explanation than poem. Beatrice, the woman who guides him through Paradise and who he loved from a distance in life, is treated as a symbol of divine grace rather than as a person, which readers hoping for a love story will find cold.

The rating

Worth the journey — for the first cantica above all. Read Hell for the architecture and the nerve; continue upward if the mood takes you. Seven hundred years on, no writer has built a more convincing world out of pure moral logic, and none has been so willing to put his own neighbors in it. Book the descent. Pack nothing.
