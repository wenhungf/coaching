Why a Warring States general still gets quoted in boardrooms — and why half of those quotes miss the point.

Walk into any strategy offsite and wait. Within an hour, someone will invoke Sun Tzu — usually the line about knowing your enemy and yourself, delivered as if it settled the argument. "The Art of War" has become the little black dress of business books: always in fashion, endlessly accessorized, and rarely read to the end.

That is a pity, because the book deserves better than its fan club.

Written around the fifth century BC and running to a mere thirteen short chapters, "The Art of War" is less a war manual than a treatise on decision-making under uncertainty. Strip away the chariots and it reads like a memo on operations, information, and self-knowledge. Sun Tzu's central claim is almost heretical to the hustle culture that worships him: the supreme skill is not to fight at all. To subdue the enemy without fighting, he argues, is the real height of skill. In corporate terms, the best deal is the one your rival concedes before the meeting even starts.

Three ideas have aged remarkably well. The first is positioning: choose the ground before the contest, so that victory is settled before the first move. The second is information asymmetry — know yourself, know your opponent, and you need not fear the outcome. The third is tempo: speed, deception, and the discipline to strike weakness rather than strength. A venture capitalist would call all three an "unfair advantage." Sun Tzu called them Tuesday.

Where the gospel wobbles

And yet the reverence is overdone. The text is gnomic to the point of a Rorschach blot: its aphorisms are so compressed that they can justify almost any decision after the fact, which is precisely why executives love them. Told to shape their strategy "like water," a manager can rationalize both a bold pivot and a cowardly retreat. A theory that explains everything predicts nothing.

Then there is the ethics problem, which the LinkedIn crowd tends to skip. Sun Tzu is candid that war rests on deception — "all warfare is based on deception" is his flattest line — and importing that logic wholesale into a marketing team, or a marriage, tends to produce exactly the results you would fear. The book is a scalpel; a great many readers wield it as a chainsaw.

Finally, a caveat about provenance. What Western readers quote is usually Lionel Giles's 1910 translation, itself an interpretation of a text layered over centuries. The crisp "Sun Tzu says" often flatters a passage that is far murkier in the original. We are quoting a translation of a compilation attributed to a general who may be half legend.

The verdict

So should it stay on the shelf? Absolutely — but as philosophy, not playbook. Read for temperament rather than tactics, "The Art of War" is a bracing corrective to the modern instinct to charge at every problem head-on. Its real lesson is patience: win the argument about the terrain, and you may never have to fight the battle. Just don't expect a 2,500-year-old general to close your Q3 pipeline. That part, unfortunately, is on you.
