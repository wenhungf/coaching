Sheepish, Furtive, Quizzical

The formal half of a 1917 phrasebook has aged badly. The half about faces and small social moments has not — and it quietly teaches the single most useful habit in English.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Phrasebook◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

Someone once owned a copy of this book and wrote a line in pencil on the flyleaf: a navy blue feeling where my heart used to be. It is not in Kleiser's lists. It is better than most of them, and it tells you what the book is really for.

Grenville Kleiser's 1917 phrasebook is a warehouse of ready-made expressions, most of them adjective plus noun. The formal ones have aged badly. The ones about people — faces, moods, small social moments — have aged remarkably well, because faces have not changed.

Faces and small moments

Kleiser gives us a sheepish look, which is the expression of someone caught doing something silly, and a furtive glance, which is quick and secretive and hopes not to be noticed. A quizzical expression is puzzled and slightly amused at once — there is no shorter way to say it. A wistful look is gently sad about something you cannot have.

Then the social ones. Polite indifference is the perfect description of someone being courteous and not caring at all. Grudging thanks are given reluctantly. Genial tolerance is the warm patience of someone putting up with you kindly. Harmless mirth is laughter that hurts nobody, and uproarious laughter is the kind that fills a room.

Situations you meet weekly

An awkward dilemma is a choice between two uncomfortable options. A curious coincidence is one that makes you pause. A golden opportunity is the chance that will not come again. A rude awakening is the unpleasant moment reality arrives — still perfectly current a hundred years on.

Two worth rescuing. A boon companion is a close, cheerful friend, and it deserves to come back. And Kleiser lists pet aversion, which is the direct ancestor of the modern pet peeve — the small thing that irritates you far more than it should.

The formal phrases aged badly. The ones about faces did not, because faces have not changed.

What the book is actually teaching

Here is the real lesson, and it is bigger than any single phrase. Kleiser does not list words. He lists pairs — adjective plus noun, always together. That is how English is actually stored by people who speak it well.

Learners tend to collect single words and then assemble them at speed, which produces combinations that are grammatically perfect and subtly wrong: a strong coincidence, a big opportunity that will not return. Native speakers are not being cleverer. They are remembering a pair they have met before. Collocation is the term for which words habitually keep company, and learning in pairs is the fastest route to sounding natural.

So the useful way to read Kleiser is not as a list of phrases to deploy. It is as evidence of which words go together.

Upgrade list

An embarrassed look → a sheepish look. A secret quick look → a furtive glance. A confused but amused face → a quizzical expression. Sad and longing → wistful. Polite but not caring → polite indifference. Thanks given unwillingly → grudging thanks. Patient and kind about it → genial tolerance. A hard choice → an awkward dilemma. A great chance → a golden opportunity. A shock about reality → a rude awakening. A small annoying thing → a pet peeve.

Two cautions

First, plenty of Kleiser has not survived. Alongside the good material sit waxwork sex, willowy nothingness and moonlight witchery. Use the book as a quarry, not a script. Second, the phrases about people are warm but old; genial and mirth in particular sound gentle and slightly literary, which is charming in writing and can sound arch in casual speech.

Try this. Describe the last time you were mildly embarrassed, using sheepish and one other pair from this page. Then notice which pair you had to think about — that is the one worth keeping.
