Rain Has Nine Names

Drizzle, downpour, cloudburst, squall — and why muggy, sultry and sweltering are three different kinds of hot.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Weather◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

Ask most learners about the weather and you get three words: hot, rainy, cold. Yet in Taiwan alone a single week can bring air so wet your shirt sticks to you, a downpour that empties the street in nine seconds, and an evening so clear you can see the mountains from the city.

English has a word for each of those. Here they are, grouped by what you actually want to say.

Rain, from lightest to heaviest

At the very lightest there is drizzle: fine, slow, barely falling. Slightly more and it is spitting, a British favorite for the few drops that warn you to go inside. A shower is brief and passing, often with blue sky behind it; showers come and go all afternoon.

Then it gets serious. A downpour is heavy and sudden. A cloudburst is a downpour that arrives all at once, as if a bag split open. Torrential rain is heavy and relentless — the word used in news reports about flooding. Driving rain is heavy rain pushed sideways by wind, which is why an umbrella stops helping. And a squall is a short violent burst of wind and rain together, usually at sea or along a coast.

Useful verbs: it is pouring (very heavy), it is bucketing down (informal, British), the rain eased off (got lighter), the rain let up (stopped for a while), the skies opened (it began suddenly and hard).

Sun and heat, which are not the same thing

A clear day has no cloud. A bright day has strong light but perhaps some cloud. A crisp day is bright and pleasantly cold — autumn, not summer. Balmy means gently warm with soft air, the best word for a pleasant evening.

Now the uncomfortable end. Muggy is warm and damp, the air heavy and still. Humid is the neutral technical word for the same wetness. Sultry is hot, still and close, with a hint of storm coming. Sweltering is oppressively hot, hot enough to slow you down. Scorching and blazing describe the sun's intensity; oppressive describes how it feels to be under it. Hazy means the air itself is milky with moisture or dust, so distance goes soft — the standard summer sky over a Taiwanese city.

The key distinction: temperature words describe the number, humidity words describe why the number feels worse. Thirty-two degrees in dry mountain air and thirty-two degrees in coastal humidity are not the same afternoon.

Temperature tells you the number. Humidity tells you why it feels worse.

Cloud, wind and cold

Cloud runs from a few wisps through scattered cloud, then overcast (completely covered, gray, flat light), then leaden, a heavy dark gray that feels like a lid. Gloomy adds the mood. Mist is thin and low; fog is thick enough to hide things; smog is fog mixed with pollution.

For wind: a breeze is light and pleasant; it is breezy. A gust is a single sudden push. Blustery means windy in strong irregular bursts. Howling and lashing are for storms. Still means no wind at all, which in humid heat is the worst possible news.

For cold: chilly is mildly unpleasant; brisk is cold but energizing; biting and bitter are painfully cold; raw is cold and damp together, which is exactly a Taipei winter. Snow words are worth having even if you rarely see them: a flurry is a brief light fall, a dusting is a thin layer on the ground, sleet is half-melted rain and snow, hail is falling ice pellets, slush is dirty half-melted snow, and black ice is a thin invisible layer on the road — the one that hurts people.

A worked example

Weak: It was very hot and then it rained a lot.

Precise: The morning was sultry and completely still, the sky a hazy white that made the far buildings dissolve. By two the cloud had gone leaden, and when the skies finally opened it came down as a proper cloudburst — torrential for twenty minutes, drains overflowing, then nothing. By evening it had eased off to a drizzle and the air was almost balmy.

Upgrade list

Very hot → sweltering, scorching, oppressive. Hot and wet → muggy, sultry. Nice and warm → balmy. A lot of rain → a downpour, a cloudburst, torrential rain. A little rain → drizzle, spitting, a passing shower. Rain stopped → the rain let up, eased off. Very cloudy → overcast, leaden. Very windy → blustery, gusty. Very cold → biting, bitter; cold and damp → raw.

Three mistakes to avoid

First, weather is uncountable: say the weather was terrible, never a terrible weather. Second, do not confuse humid with wet — humid describes air, wet describes surfaces. Third, in English you say it is raining, with an empty it; there is no subject to supply, and adding one (the sky is raining) sounds odd.

Try this. Describe the weather outside your window right now in three sentences: one for the sky, one for the air, one for what it makes you want to do.
