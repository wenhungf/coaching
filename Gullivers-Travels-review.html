Four Failed States: A Diplomatic Assessment

Jonathan Swift’s traveler washes up in four countries and files four reports. Three hundred years later, they still read like a briefing on this morning’s news.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Jonathan Swift, Gulliver’s Travels◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

The subject of this assessment came ashore in November 1699 and woke to find himself pinned flat to a beach by several hundred citizens six inches tall, every one of them convinced he was a national security problem.

Jonathan Swift's traveler, a ship's surgeon named Lemuel Gulliver, makes four voyages and returns with four reports: a kingdom of tiny people, a kingdom of giants, a flying island of distracted scientists, and a country where reasonable horses rule over filthy human-shaped animals. Three centuries on, the reports still read like the sharpest political briefing you will see this year.

Lilliput: politics in miniature

The people of Lilliput stand six inches high and take themselves extremely seriously. Government posts are awarded by rope-dancing: candidates perform on a tightrope, and whoever jumps highest gets the job. The court splits into rival parties over the height of their shoe heels. And the empire has been at war for years with its neighbor over which end of a boiled egg should be cracked first — Big-Endians against Little-Endians, thousands dead over breakfast. Swift is describing the political parties and religious wars of his own century, and he is not being subtle. He is being exact.

Brobdingnag: the view from above

Reverse the scale and the joke turns on Gulliver. Among the giants of Brobdingnag he becomes a pet, carried in a box, shown at fairs, and fought off by a wasp the size of a bird. He describes European government to the king with enormous pride; the king listens carefully, asks precise questions, and concludes that Gulliver's countrymen are the most pernicious race of little odious vermin ever to crawl on the earth. Gulliver, undeterred, offers to teach him how to make gunpowder. The king recoils in horror — and the offer, not the horror, is Swift's point.

A war over which end of an egg to crack is still the truest thing ever written about politics.

Laputa: research without a purpose

Laputa is an island that flies, held up by a giant magnet, and its king can park it above a rebellious city to block out the sun — or, if that fails to persuade, lower it onto the rooftops. On the ground below, the funded researchers of the Grand Academy work to extract sunbeams from cucumbers, build houses starting with the roof, and soften marble to make pillows. Swift was aiming at the scientific societies of his day. Anyone who has read a modern grant application will find the aim has not drifted much.

The fourth voyage, where the report turns

Then the tone changes, and the book stops being funny. Among the Houyhnhnms — horses of perfect reason — Gulliver also meets the Yahoos: greedy, filthy, violent creatures who are unmistakably human. He decides the horses are right about us. He sails home and cannot bear the smell of his own wife and children; he buys two horses and spends his afternoons talking to them instead. This is not a children's book, whatever the picture-book versions suggest, and readers who arrive expecting one are in for a shock.

Assessment

The flaws are real. Gulliver himself is a lens rather than a person — he exists to measure, not to feel. The third voyage sprawls. Some episodes are deliberately coarse, including a palace fire that Gulliver extinguishes by the least dignified means available. And the fourth voyage leaves a bruise: readers have argued for three hundred years over whether Swift shared Gulliver's disgust with humanity or was diagnosing it. Read it anyway, and read the whole thing. No one since has cut this close to the bone, and nobody has ever needed fewer words to do it than a war fought over an egg.
