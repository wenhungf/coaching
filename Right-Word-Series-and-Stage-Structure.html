Was That Twist Earned?

Pilot, arc, cliffhanger, filler — the parts of a series and a play, and the one pair of words that carries most English criticism.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Screen and stage◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

Everyone has had this conversation. A friend asks what you thought of the show, and the honest answer is that the first half was great, the middle dragged, and the ending annoyed you — but what comes out is "it was okay, the last part was a bit weird."

Screen and stage have a precise vocabulary for their own parts. Once you have it, you can say exactly where a story lost you.

The units, from smallest to largest

On stage, a play is divided into acts, and each act into scenes. A scene usually changes when the place or the time changes; an act is a larger movement, and the gap between them is the interval in British usage, the intermission in American.

On television, the unit is the episode, and a run of episodes is a season — or a series in British usage, which causes constant confusion, since Americans use series for the whole show. The first episode is the pilot, made to test whether the idea works. The last of a season is the finale, and the very last of everything is the series finale.

Two more worth knowing. A cold open is a scene that runs before the opening titles, dropping you straight into the action. A bottle episode is one shot cheaply in a single location, usually to save money elsewhere in the season.

How a story is built

An arc is the shape of a change across time: a character arc is how a person is different by the end, and a season arc is the story running underneath the individual episodes. A subplot is a smaller story running alongside the main one. Exposition is the information the audience needs, and clumsy exposition is the great sin of early episodes — two characters explaining to each other things they both already know.

Then the machinery of surprise. Foreshadowing is an early hint that only makes sense later. A payoff is the moment that hint delivers. A twist reverses what you believed. A cliffhanger ends an episode mid-danger so you keep watching. A red herring is a false clue planted to mislead you. And filler is material that exists only to fill time, advancing nothing.

A twist that was earned feels inevitable. One that was not feels like cheating.

The most useful pair of words

English criticism turns on one distinction: whether a moment was earned. An earned twist is one the story prepared you for — looking back, the clues were there, and the surprise feels inevitable. An unearned twist arrives from nowhere to shock you, and it feels like the writer cheating. The extreme case has its own name: a deus ex machina, where an outside force appears at the last moment and solves everything the characters could not.

Two neighbors. If a story telegraphed its ending, it made the outcome obvious far too early. If it was contrived, the events were forced into place to suit the plot rather than arising from the characters. These three words will carry most of the criticism you ever need.

Pacing and where shows fail

Pacing is the speed at which a story moves. When a middle section slows without purpose, it drags or sags. When too much happens too quickly, it feels rushed. A show that loses its way in a later season has jumped the shark, an idiom from a 1970s television episode in which a character literally waterskied over a shark, now used for the moment a series becomes absurd.

Upgrade list

The first one → the pilot. The last one → the finale. The story across the whole thing → the season arc. How the character changes → the character arc. A surprise at the end → a twist. The surprise made no sense → it was unearned. I guessed it too early → it was telegraphed. Too convenient → contrived. Boring middle → the pacing sags. Nothing happened in that one → it was filler. It got ridiculous → it jumped the shark.

Two things to get right

First, the British and American senses of series are genuinely different: British series two equals American season two. If you are talking to people from both, say season and be understood everywhere. Second, a plot hole is a gap in logic; a twist you disliked is not a plot hole. Keeping that distinction makes your criticism much harder to dismiss.

Try this. Take the last thing you watched and write three sentences: one on the pacing, one on whether the ending was earned, and one on the single episode you would cut.
