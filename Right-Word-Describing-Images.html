The Grammar of an Image

Most people can only say a photograph is nice. Here are the words for what it is actually doing — and why one you dislike is not working.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Visual communication◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

Somebody shows you a photograph and asks what you think of it. You look at it for a moment and say: it's nice. The lighting is good. And that is the whole review, because the words for everything else were never taught.

Images have a grammar. Once you can name the parts, you can say exactly what a picture is doing — and, just as usefully, exactly why one you dislike is not working.

Where things sit

Start with depth. Anything closest to the viewer is in the foreground; the far distance is the background; anything between them sits in the middle ground. The thing the image is actually about is the subject.

How those parts are arranged is the composition. If the subject sits dead in the middle, it is centered. If it sits a third of the way in, following the old photographers' guide of dividing the frame into nine, it follows the rule of thirds. Empty space around the subject is negative space, and it is not wasted — it gives the eye somewhere to rest. Lines that pull your attention toward the subject, like a road or a fence, are leading lines. The place your eye lands first is the focal point.

Two more you will need constantly. Framing is what the photographer chose to include and where the edges fall; cropping is cutting the picture down afterward. If someone's arm is cut off at the edge, that arm is cropped out.

Juxtaposition: the most useful word here

Juxtaposition means placing two things side by side so that the contrast between them creates meaning. A shining new tower next to a collapsing house is a juxtaposition; so is a sharp subject against a blurred crowd. Nothing is stated, but the reader gets the point.

This is the word most learners are missing, and it does an enormous amount of work in one move: the juxtaposition of the child's toy and the empty street makes the photograph unsettling. Note the pattern: the juxtaposition of X and Y + what it does.

Name the parts, and you can finally say why a picture works.

Sharpness and blur

Depth of field is how much of the image is in focus front-to-back. In a shallow depth of field only a thin slice is sharp and everything else falls away softly — a portrait with a creamy background. A deep depth of field keeps everything crisp, from the flowers at your feet to the mountain behind.

The vocabulary of softness matters. Blurred is the general word. Out of focus means the camera focused elsewhere. Soft is gentler and often deliberate. Hazy suggests something in the air — mist, dust, heat. Motion blur is the streak left by something moving during the exposure, and it is how a photograph shows speed.

A worked example

Here is the same photograph described twice.

Weak: A nice photo of a man on a bike. The background is blurry. Good colors.

Precise: The subject is a cyclist, framed slightly off-center against a wall of market stalls that dissolve into color behind him. The shallow depth of field isolates him completely; only his face and hands are sharp. Leading lines in the wet road pull the eye from the bottom-left corner straight to the front wheel, and the juxtaposition of his stillness and the streaking motion blur of a passing bus makes the picture feel like a held breath.

Upgrade list

Trade the vague word for the exact one. Blurry → out of focus, soft, hazy, or motion-blurred, depending on the cause. The thing in the picture → the subject. The stuff behind → the background. Where things are → the composition. Empty bit → negative space. It draws your eye → it is the focal point. Cut off → cropped. Two things next to each other → a juxtaposition.

Two mistakes to avoid

First: foreground is one word, and it means nearest to you, not most important — a subject can sit in the background. Second: focus is not the same as focal point. Focus is about sharpness; the focal point is where the eye goes. A subject can be the focal point while being slightly out of focus, and that is often a deliberate and beautiful choice.

Now try it. Take any photograph on your phone and describe it in three sentences, using foreground, subject, composition, and one word for blur. The third sentence should say what the arrangement makes you feel.
