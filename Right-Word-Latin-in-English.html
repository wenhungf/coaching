You Already Write Latin Every Day

e.g. or i.e.? De facto or de jure? The Latin layer of English, sorted out — including the plurals that catch people, and the point where it starts sounding evasive.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Loanwords◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

You already write Latin every day. Every time you type e.g., etc., vs. or a.m. you are using a two-thousand-year-old language in abbreviated form — and if you have ever hesitated over whether to write e.g. or i.e., you have felt exactly why this article exists.

English took Latin twice: once through law, church and scholarship in the Middle Ages, and again during the Renaissance, when writers borrowed heavily to sound learned. The result is a permanent second layer of vocabulary sitting above the plain English words — and knowing which layer you are in is most of what people mean by register.

The abbreviations, settled once

These four cause more errors than anything else in professional writing.

e.g. is exempli gratia, for example. Use it when what follows is a sample, not the whole list: bring something warm, e.g. a jacket. i.e. is id est, that is. Use it when what follows restates or defines: bring one item of outerwear, i.e. a jacket. The trick that works: e.g. = example, i.e. = in essence.

etc. is et cetera, and the rest. Never write and etc., because the and is already inside it. et al. is et alii, and others, and it is for people, not things — a paper by Chen et al., never a list of fruit et al. Note that only al. takes the period, because et is a complete word.

The ones that carry weight

These appear in contracts, reports and serious journalism, and using them correctly is a visible signal of precision.

A caveat is a warning or condition attached to a statement. The status quo is the existing state of affairs. Per se means in itself, and it is for isolating one part of a claim: the delay is not the problem per se; the silence is. De facto means in practice, whatever the rules say; de jure means by law. Ad hoc means arranged for this one purpose, not planned in advance. Bona fide means genuine and in good faith. A quid pro quo is something given in exchange for something else. Vice versa reverses the two things just mentioned. And pro rata means divided in proportion.

Two you will meet in argument. A non sequitur is a conclusion that does not follow from what came before. Ad hominem attacks the person rather than the argument.

Latin is the second floor of English. Know which floor you are standing on.

Plurals, where people slip

Latin nouns brought their own plurals, and English is slowly digesting them. Some are still strictly observed: one criterion, several criteria; one phenomenon, several phenomena; one alumnus, many alumni. Data and media were originally plural and are now widely treated as singular in ordinary use; in scientific writing, the data are remains standard. And agenda was a Latin plural that became an English singular so completely that we now say agendas.

The safe rule: in formal or academic writing, keep criteria, phenomena and analyses as plurals. Elsewhere, follow ordinary usage and do not correct people at dinner.

A worked example

Overloaded: Per se, the ad hoc committee's de facto status quo vis-a-vis the criteria is, ipso facto, a non sequitur.

Correct: The committee was formed ad hoc, so its authority is de facto rather than de jure. That is a caveat worth stating before we agree to anything.

Two Latin phrases in a paragraph read as precise. Six read as someone hiding behind vocabulary.

Upgrade list

For example → e.g. In other words → i.e. In reality, not officially → de facto. Officially, by law → de jure. Made up on the spot for one job → ad hoc. A condition attached → a caveat. How things are now → the status quo. In itself → per se. A favor for a favor → a quid pro quo. The other way round → vice versa. That does not follow → a non sequitur.

Three mistakes to avoid

First, per se is two words and is not spelled per say. Second, e.g. and i.e. take periods and are followed by a comma in American usage. Third — and this one matters most — do not stack these. Latin raises the register of a sentence, and a sentence with three of them stops sounding authoritative and starts sounding evasive.

Try this. Take a work email you sent recently and find one place where a caveat, per se or ad hoc would have made you clearer. Then check you have not used e.g. where you meant i.e.
