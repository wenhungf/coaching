Literature’s Greatest Road

An orphan who belongs everywhere and nowhere walks 1,500 miles up the Grand Trunk Road with a Tibetan monk. The road is the finest thing in English travel writing — and the politics are complicated.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Rudyard Kipling, Kim◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

Somewhere past Umballa the road fills up: bullock carts, a marriage procession with drums, a squad of soldiers, holy men walking barefoot, moneylenders, a woman scolding from a curtained litter, and dust over all of it. A boy sits on the edge of it watching, and decides this is the finest thing in the world.

He is right, and so is the book. Rudyard Kipling published Kim in 1901: the story of Kimball O'Hara, the orphaned son of an Irish soldier, growing up wild in the streets of Lahore, so brown from the sun and so fluent in the local languages that nobody takes him for a white child. He attaches himself to an old Tibetan lama who is searching for a sacred river, and together they walk out onto the Grand Trunk Road. Along the way, British intelligence discovers that a boy who can pass unnoticed anywhere is extremely useful.

The road is the main character

The Grand Trunk Road runs some fifteen hundred miles across the north of the subcontinent, and Kipling gives it the best pages in the book. It is a river of people: every caste, every trade, every religion moving in both directions at once, arguing and cooking and sleeping at the side. Nothing in Victorian fiction is so crowded with life, and almost nothing is so generous about it. He notices the smells. He notices what people eat, how they haggle, which insult they choose.

Every caste, every trade, every religion, moving in both directions at once.

Two journeys, one road

The structure is the clever part. Two quests share the same route and never quite touch. The lama walks toward a river that will wash away sin; Kim walks into the Great Game, the long intelligence contest between Britain and Russia over the mountain routes into India, carrying messages and learning to memorize a tray of objects at a glance. One journey is spiritual and slow, the other is worldly and quick.

What makes the book ache is that the boy loves them both. He is a spy who genuinely reveres his teacher, and the closing pages force a choice he never wanted. It is also a real portrait of a child raised between cultures, at home in a bazaar and never quite at home anywhere, asking again and again a question the plot will not answer neatly: what am I?

The problem the book cannot escape

Kipling was the great writer of the British Empire, and this is an imperial novel. India is described with immense affection and immense confidence — it is observed, catalogued, admired, and administered. The Indian characters are vivid and often magnificent, but the machinery of British rule sits there unquestioned, as natural as the weather. Kim's gift, in the end, is put to work for that machinery.

Readers have argued about this for a century, and the argument is worth having rather than settling. It is possible to find the road scenes among the finest travel writing in English and still notice who is holding the pen, and for whom the intelligence is gathered.

Where it slows

Practical warnings: the spycraft plot is thin, and it more or less evaporates whenever the road gets interesting. Kipling drops in Hindustani and Anglo-Indian words with no explanation at all, and a modern edition with notes helps enormously. The final chapters go inward and quiet after a very busy book, which some readers find beautiful and others find a stall.

The verdict

Read it for the road. There is no better account in English of what it felt like to walk across northern India in the 1890s — the noise, the food, the sheer human traffic — and no more affecting portrait of an old man and a sharp-witted boy looking after each other. Go with your eyes open about what the book assumes. Then let it take you out onto the Grand Trunk Road, where, as Kim says, there is all the world.
