Props, Blocking, Delivery

Set, backdrop, score, blocking, chemistry — the vocabulary for everything you notice in a production without quite being able to name it.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Screen and stage◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

Something is wrong with the scene and you cannot say what. The acting seems fine, the story makes sense, and yet it feels cheap, or airless, or oddly flat. Almost always the problem is in the parts nobody is looking at directly.

This is the vocabulary for those parts — what is built, what is lit, what is worn, and how it is performed.

What is built and what is carried

The set is everything constructed for the scene to happen in. The backdrop is the painted or projected surface at the very back. Props, short for properties, are the objects the actors handle: a cup, a letter, a gun. Set dressing is everything placed to make the space look lived in but never touched.

This distinction is worth having, because it lets you praise precisely. The set was fine but the props were wrong is a real observation: it means the space looked right and the objects in it did not belong to those people.

Light and sound

Lighting design is how a production uses light to shape mood. A spotlight isolates one figure. A wash covers the stage in even color. Practical lights are the ones visible in the scene itself — a lamp, a candle — and they are what make an interior feel real. When a stage goes to blackout, everything cuts to dark at once.

On the sound side: the score is the music written for the production, and a soundtrack is the collection of songs used. Sound design covers everything else — footsteps, rain, the hum of a room. A scene described as airless usually has no ambient sound at all, and the silence reads as artificial.

Bodies in space

Blocking is the planned movement of actors around the stage or set — who stands where, and when they cross. Bad blocking is why a scene can feel stiff even when the lines are good. Costume design covers what people wear, and continuity is the job of keeping details consistent between shots, so a glass is not full, then empty, then full again.

Two effects words. Practical effects are made physically in front of the camera: real fire, real rain, real prosthetics. CGI is added by computer afterward. Audiences often prefer practical effects because light behaves correctly on real objects, which is the same depth problem that makes some images look flat.

Bad blocking is why a scene feels stiff even when the writing is good.

Performance

Casting is the choice of actors. Someone miscast is simply wrong for the part, however good they are. Chemistry is whether two performers convince you of a relationship, and it either exists or does not. An ensemble is a cast working as a balanced group rather than around one star, and a scene-stealer is the minor character everyone remembers.

For the acting itself: delivery is how a line is spoken. Range is how far an actor can travel from their usual register. Understated performance holds back, and it is usually a compliment. Wooden means stiff and lifeless. Hammy or over the top means exaggerated, chewing the scenery. Melodramatic is the close relative you will hear most often, though it points slightly differently: hammy is about the acting, melodramatic is about pitching the emotion far above what the moment deserves. A performance described as restrained is doing a great deal with very little.

Upgrade list

The stuff on stage → the set and the props. The background → the backdrop. The lights were good → the lighting design was precise. The music → the score. The way they moved around → the blocking. Wrong actor for the role → miscast. They didn't seem like a couple → no chemistry. The way they said the line → the delivery. Stiff acting → wooden. Too much acting → hammy. Quiet but powerful acting → understated.

Two things to get right

First, props are objects that get handled; scenery is what they stand in front of. Calling a chair a prop is fine if someone sits on it during the scene, and wrong if it is simply there. Second, understated and wooden can look similar on paper but mean opposite things — understated is deliberate control, wooden is an absence of life. If you are unsure which you saw, ask whether the stillness was doing something.

Try this. Describe one scene you loved in three sentences: one on the set or props, one on the lighting or sound, and one on a single actor's delivery.
