Ten Years, One Commute

A five-hundred-mile trip home. Ten years in transit, one surviving passenger, and a delay the claimant blames entirely on the weather. We review the file.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Homer, The Odyssey◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

The complaint before us concerns a journey of roughly five hundred miles that took the claimant ten years, cost him his entire crew, and involved a delay caused, he insists, by the weather service itself.

The facts are these. Odysseus, king of a small Greek island called Ithaca, sails for home after ten years at war in Troy. Almost nothing goes to plan. He is blown off course, imprisoned by a witch, held for seven years by a sea nymph who wants to marry him, and finally washed up alone with nothing. Meanwhile his wife Penelope is holding their house against a hundred and eight men who have moved in, are eating his food, and expect her to marry one of them. This is Homer's poem, composed around the eighth century BC, and it remains the greatest travel-disruption story ever told.

Itinerary and incidents

The outbound leg was uneventful. The return was not. At the first stop the crew are drugged with lotus fruit and stop wanting to leave. At the second, a one-eyed giant named Polyphemus eats several of them, and Odysseus escapes by blinding him and clinging to the underside of a sheep. He is then handed a bag containing every wind except the one blowing him home; his men, convinced it holds treasure, open it in sight of Ithaca and blow the ship all the way back.

After that: a witch who turns the crew into pigs, a strait guarded on one side by a monster and on the other by a whirlpool, and finally a beach where the last survivors eat cattle they had been explicitly warned not to touch. Their ship is destroyed. Odysseus alone reaches land, and spends seven more years on an island with the nymph Calypso before anyone lets him go.

Grounds for complaint

He blames the gods, and the record supports him: Poseidon, god of the sea, spends the entire poem obstructing him. But an honest assessment of liability must note that Odysseus is a poor captain. He raids a town on the way home for no reason and loses men. He shouts his own name at the blinded giant while sailing away, which is how Poseidon learns whom to punish. He fails to tell his crew what is in the bag. Every single man under his command dies; he is the only one who makes it back. Any airline with that record would not be operating.

The hospitality clause

Underneath the monsters, the poem runs on a rule the Greeks called xenia: a host must feed and shelter a stranger before asking who he is, and the guest must not abuse the house. It works like an ancient review system, and the plot is a series of ratings. The good hosts prosper. The giant who eats his visitors and the suitors who devour another man's household both meet the same ending, and it is not a refund.

Where the service falls short

Modern readers should be warned about the repetition. Homer was composing for performance, so the same ready-made phrases return again and again — dawn is rosy-fingered every single morning — and whole speeches are repeated word for word by messengers. It was a memory aid for a poet working without a script; on the page it can drag. The middle books slow to a crawl, and the revenge at the end is far bloodier than most people expect from a story often sold to children.

Finding

Complaint upheld, with contributory fault on the claimant's part. Three thousand years on, nothing has bettered it: the homesickness, the lying, the disguises, the dog who recognizes his master and then dies. And Penelope, quietly running a twenty-year defense of her own, is the better strategist of the two. Read it for her as much as for him.
