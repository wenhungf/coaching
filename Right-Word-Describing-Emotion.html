Nine Words for a Bad Week

Apprehensive, deflated, indignant, mortified, bittersweet — emotion vocabulary organized by the feeling underneath, plus the three-part sentence that makes it sound fluent.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Emotion◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

Here is a test. Something at work went badly last week, and a colleague asks how you feel about it. If the honest answer is "bad," you have three or four English words available. If the honest answer is that you are embarrassed but also quietly relieved it is over, you need about nine.

Emotion vocabulary is where fluent speakers separate from advanced ones, because the feelings are not simple and the words for them are not interchangeable. What follows is organized by the feeling underneath, not by the alphabet.

When something bad might happen

Worried is the general word. Anxious is heavier and more physical — it lives in the body. Apprehensive is specific: you are expecting one particular thing and dreading it. Uneasy is vaguer, a sense that something is wrong without knowing what. On edge means you are so tense that a small noise would make you jump. Dread is the heavy, slow certainty that something bad is coming.

Notice the difference in shape. You are anxious about an exam, apprehensive about meeting your partner's parents, uneasy in a room where two people have just argued.

When something bad has happened

Disappointed is the loss of a good outcome you expected. Deflated is what happens to your energy afterward — the air going out of you. Crushed is far heavier. Dejected is low and beaten. Despondent has lost hope entirely.

Separately: frustrated is being blocked from doing something you could do. Exasperated is frustration plus impatience, usually with a person. Indignant is anger at unfairness, and it is a righteous word — you are indignant on behalf of what is right, not merely annoyed. Resentful is anger that has settled in and stayed, quietly, for a long time.

Indignant is anger at unfairness. Resentful is anger that stayed.

When you got it wrong

This family is subtle and English speakers use it constantly. Embarrassed is the hot public feeling of having looked foolish. Sheepish is its mild, almost charming version — caught doing something silly, and half-smiling about it. Mortified is extreme: embarrassment so total you want to leave the country.

Then the moral end. Guilty is knowing you did something wrong. Ashamed is heavier and about who you are rather than what you did. Remorseful is guilt with the wish to make it right. Contrite is remorse expressed and offered as an apology.

The distinction that matters most: guilty about what I did, ashamed of who I was being. Mixing them changes the size of the confession.

When it is over

Relieved is the release of tension when a threat passes. Vindicated is being proved right after being doubted. Wistful is gently sad about something you cannot have again — usually the past. Bittersweet holds happiness and sadness at once, which is why it is the right word for a graduation or a last day.

And the quiet positives, which learners rarely have. Content is settled and satisfied, without excitement. Gratified is pleased that something you did was recognized. Buoyant is light and optimistic. Elated is high, bright joy. Touched is moved by someone's kindness, and it is the exact word for a small gift that mattered.

How to build the sentence

Precision comes from structure as much as vocabulary. English gives you three moves.

Name the trigger: apprehensive about the meeting, indignant at the decision, touched by what she said. Grade the intensity: slightly, a little, quite, deeply, utterly. Or admit the mixture, which is where fluency really shows: relieved, but also a little deflated. Real feelings are usually two things at once, and saying so is more accurate than choosing the stronger one and pretending.

Upgrade list

Very worried → anxious, apprehensive, on edge. Something feels off → uneasy. Sad and out of energy → deflated. Very sad and hopeless → despondent. Angry because it's unfair → indignant. Still angry after a long time → resentful. Annoyed by a person again and again → exasperated. So embarrassed → mortified. A bit embarrassed → sheepish. Sorry and want to fix it → remorseful. Happy and sad together → bittersweet. Happy someone was kind → touched. Calm and okay → content.

Three things to get right

First, English distinguishes the person from the thing: you are bored, the lecture is boring; you are frightened, the film is frightening. Saying I am boring tells people something else entirely. Second, upset is not a synonym for angry — it covers sad, shaken and distressed, and native speakers use it when the exact feeling is unclear or too private to specify. Third, do not over-reach for the dramatic word. Saying you are devastated about a delayed train costs you the word when you actually need it.

Try this. Think of the last thing that went wrong for you and write two sentences: one naming the feeling and its trigger, one admitting the second feeling underneath. The second sentence is the one that sounds fluent.
