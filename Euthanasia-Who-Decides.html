Who Decides When Suffering Ends?

A Dutch report recorded the first euthanasia of a child under twelve. Behind one line of statistics sits a century-old argument — and a set of psychological reflexes that make it almost impossible to have calmly.

InkQuest Reading Room◆Feature · Euthanasia and the psychology of dying◆Level B2–C1◆~9 min read

In June 2026 a single sentence appeared in a Dutch government report that almost nobody reads. The committee that reviews end-of-life decisions had received, in late 2025, its first notification concerning a child between the ages of one and twelve. No name. No illness. No age. One line, and then the rest of the statistics.

It was the first case under regulations the Netherlands introduced in 2024, and it reopened an argument that has run for a century and shows no sign of ending. Almost everyone who joins that argument is certain. Almost nobody agrees on what the words even mean.

Four different things, one word

Clear thinking here depends entirely on definitions, because the public conversation constantly mixes up practices that doctors treat as completely separate.

Euthanasia means a doctor actively ending a patient's life, usually by administering a lethal drug. Assisted suicide means the doctor supplies the means and the patient performs the final act themselves — the distinction that separates the Dutch model from the Swiss one, and from most American states. Withdrawing treatment means stopping a ventilator or a feeding tube and allowing an underlying illness to kill the patient; this is legal almost everywhere and is not classified as euthanasia at all. Palliative sedation means using drugs to keep a dying patient unconscious and comfortable, accepting that this may shorten life slightly, without that being the aim.

The distance between the second and third of those is where a great deal of the argument actually lives. Critics say it is a distinction without a difference. Defenders say intention is the whole of medicine.

What the Dutch law actually says

The Netherlands legalized euthanasia for adults in 2002, the first country in the world to do so. The criteria are demanding: the request must be voluntary and well-considered, the suffering must be unbearable with no prospect of improvement, a second independent doctor must agree, and every case is reported afterwards to a review committee.

For young people, the law built a strange staircase. Minors aged twelve and over could request euthanasia, with parental involvement required below sixteen. Newborns under one year could have their lives ended in extreme cases under the Groningen Protocol, on their parents' decision. Between those two floors sat a gap: children aged one to twelve, old enough to have outgrown the infant rules and too young to qualify under the adult ones.

A study commissioned by the government found doctors and parents who believed some children were dying badly inside that gap. The 2024 regulation closed it. Technically the act remains a criminal offence; what changed is that prosecutors will not charge a doctor who follows the criteria — terminal illness, hopeless and unbearable suffering, no reasonable alternative, parental consent. Officials estimated it might apply to five to ten children a year. In the first full period of operation, it applied to one.

Almost everyone in this argument is certain. Almost nobody agrees on what the words mean.

The case for

Supporters begin with autonomy: an adult who owns their life owns its ending, and a state that forces a competent person to endure agony is not protecting them but overruling them. They point out that we already accept death as an outcome of medical choices — we withdraw ventilators, we sedate people into unconsciousness — and argue that refusing the faster route is squeamishness rather than principle.

They make an argument from honesty, too. Where assisted dying is illegal, some doctors help anyway, quietly and without oversight; legalization replaces a hidden practice with a documented one. And they argue that the reality of some deaths is not adequately captured by the phrase "palliative care can manage it": there are cancers of the head and neck, degenerative diseases, and forms of suffering that resist every drug available.

For children specifically, the case is narrower and harder. Its defenders do not claim a small child can weigh such a decision. They argue that a child dying in pain that cannot be relieved is already beyond rescue, that parents and doctors make life-and-death decisions for children constantly — to operate, to stop treatment, to resuscitate or not — and that a rule which allows a doctor to withdraw a feeding tube but not to act directly is drawing its line by squeamishness rather than by mercy.

The case against

Opponents start with the character of the act itself. Medicine's oldest instruction is to do no harm, and a profession that kills patients, however kindly, becomes something other than what it was. Once that door opens, they argue, it does not stay where it was put: the Dutch law began with terminal adults and now covers psychiatric suffering, dementia, and children, each expansion justified by the last. This is the slippery slope objection, and its force is that it does not require anyone to act in bad faith.

Then the argument about pressure. The sick and disabled frequently feel they are a burden; a legal option to die can quietly become an expectation to die, particularly where care is expensive and families are exhausted. Disability rights organizations have been among the most consistent opponents, and their objection is not religious — it is that a society which offers assisted death more readily than it offers home care, wheelchairs and personal assistants is not expanding choice but narrowing it.

Some argue that unbearable suffering is a claim about the failure of palliative care rather than an argument for killing, and that resources should go to fixing the first problem. Others hold a straightforwardly religious position: life is not ours to end. And for children, opponents make the sharpest point available — a child of six cannot consent, so this is not assisted dying at all. It is a decision made for someone by other people, and calling it a mercy does not change who is holding the syringe.

Why children are the hardest case

Every framework for assisted dying rests on competence: the capacity to understand a situation, weigh alternatives, and choose. Children are precisely where that foundation gives way.

Developmental researchers describe children's understanding of death as four separate ideas that arrive at different times: that death is irreversible, that it is universal, that the body stops functioning, and that it has physical causes. Most children hold all four by around seven. Below that, a child may understand that they are very sick and frightened, and may not grasp that being dead has no morning after.

This produces a genuine dilemma rather than a rhetorical one. If a small child cannot consent, then either their suffering is treated as beyond help, or someone else decides. Belgium took one route in 2014, removing the age limit but requiring that the child have what the law calls capacity for discernment, assessed by a psychiatrist — which in practice restricts it to older, gravely ill minors, and has produced only a handful of cases. The Netherlands took the other, treating the youngest children as a matter of parental and medical judgment. Canada studied the question of mature minors and declined. Most countries have not gone near it.

What we do with the thought of dying

Underneath the legal argument sits a psychological one, and it explains why these debates get so hot so quickly.

The anthropologist Ernest Becker argued in 1973 that awareness of our own death is so intolerable that civilization is largely a machine for not thinking about it: our projects, our nations, our religions and our children are ways of belonging to something that outlasts us. Psychologists later built this into terror management theory, which produced an unsettling body of experiments. Remind people of their mortality, even briefly, and they defend their own worldview more fiercely and judge outsiders more harshly. In one well-known study, judges asked to reflect on their own death set markedly higher bail in a hypothetical case than colleagues who had not been reminded.

If that is right, an argument about euthanasia is never only an argument about policy. It is conducted by people whose defences have just been activated, which is one reason both sides so often accuse the other of monstrousness.

The other framework everyone half-knows is Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's five stages — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — published in 1969. It is worth knowing what she actually claimed. She was describing patients told they were dying, not the bereaved, and she never proposed a fixed sequence that everyone completes. The popular version, in which a griever advances neatly through five levels and finishes, has weak evidence behind it and does real harm, because people conclude they are grieving incorrectly. Researchers now favor models of oscillation: mourners swing between confronting the loss and getting on with life, and both movements are necessary.

Remind people of death and they defend their worldview more fiercely. This debate is conducted by people whose defences have just gone up.

The stories we tell about it

Fiction has been arguing about this for as long as parliaments have, and where it lands is revealing.

Clint Eastwood's Million Dollar Baby ends with a trainer ending the life of a paralysed fighter who asks him to, and disability organizations protested it fiercely for treating paralysis as a fate worse than death. The same objection met Me Before You a decade later, whose wealthy quadriplegic hero travels to Switzerland to die; campaigners noted that a story about choice had somehow produced only one possible choice. Alejandro Amenábar's The Sea Inside dramatized the real thirty-year campaign of Ramón Sampedro, and Jodi Picoult's My Sister's Keeper put a minor in court asking to control her own medical fate — the child-autonomy question in its most direct form.

The dystopias run the other way, and they are all versions of the slippery slope: Logan's Run, where the state kills everyone at thirty; Soylent Green, where an exhausted old man checks into a government suite to die to soothing music and film of a vanished world. What such stories fear is never a doctor's compassion. It is a bureaucracy that finds compassion convenient.

Then there is Terry Pratchett, who made the argument in his own voice. Diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's, he campaigned openly for assisted dying and presented a documentary in which he accompanied a man to a Swiss clinic and stayed with him. Pratchett wanted the option and, as it happened, never used it; he died of his illness in 2015. It is an unusually clean illustration of the supporters' central claim — that the value of the option lies in having it.

And some of the best stories about mortality are not about hastening it at all. Coco builds an entire afterlife on the idea that the dead persist while someone remembers them. Grave of the Fireflies refuses to make dying meaningful. Margaret Edson's play Wit puts a professor of seventeenth-century poetry through terminal cancer and lets her discover that erudition is no protection whatsoever.

Where that leaves us

The Dutch case will be reviewed, as every case is, and the file will probably stay closed. Five to ten children a year was the estimate; one was the reality. Both sides have already read that number as proof they were right — that the safeguards are working, or that a threshold has been crossed and the numbers will climb.

What seems clear is that the argument cannot be settled by insisting the other side is wicked. The strongest case for assisted dying is not a contempt for life, and the strongest case against it is not an indifference to suffering. Anyone who cannot state their opponent's position in a form that opponent would accept has not yet joined the argument.

Questions to argue about

Is there a real moral difference between switching off a ventilator and administering a drug? If not, which practice should change?

Does the slippery slope objection describe something that has actually happened in the Netherlands since 2002, or something people feared would happen?

If a child cannot consent, can this be called euthanasia at all — and if it is a decision made by others, does that make it worse, or simply honest about what all paediatric medicine involves?

Disability campaigners say the problem is the surrounding society, not the law itself. What would have to be true about a country's healthcare before assisted dying could be considered a free choice?

Terror management theory suggests you will argue this more rigidly right after being reminded of your own death. Does knowing that change how you want to argue it?
