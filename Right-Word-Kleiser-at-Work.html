Fifteen Thousand Phrases, Sorted

A 1917 handbook promised to make anyone sound educated. A century on, some of it still belongs in your next email — and some of it belongs in a museum.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Phrasebook◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

In 1917 a public-speaking instructor at Yale published a book containing fifteen thousand ready-made phrases, arranged alphabetically, for anyone who wanted to sound like an educated person. It is a strange, magnificent object. Most of it is unusable today. Some of it is pure gold, and the gold is worth digging out.

Grenville Kleiser's Fifteen Thousand Useful Phrases is long out of copyright and free to read online. What follows is a working sort: the phrases that still earn their place in an office, the ones that need a trim, and the ones that died and deserved to.

Still gold

These appear in Kleiser's lists and are still exactly right a century later.

A salient feature is the one that stands out and matters most — the single most useful upgrade on this page, because meetings are full of people saying the main thing. A foregone conclusion is a result decided before the discussion started. Mitigating circumstances are facts that make a failure more understandable, and extenuating circumstances are the same idea in a more formal register.

A tacit assumption is one everybody is working from but nobody has said aloud. A cogent statement is clear, logical and convincing. A pertinent question is directly relevant, not merely interesting. A valid objection is one that actually holds. Incontrovertible proof cannot be argued with, and a damaging admission is the moment someone concedes something that hurts their own case.

Two more that work hard. Perfunctory means done without care, purely to satisfy a requirement — the exact word for a review nobody meant. Cursory means quick and surface-level: a cursory glance at the figures. Both let you criticize an effort precisely rather than calling it bad.

Most meetings are full of people saying "the main thing." The word is salient.

The one that traps everyone

Kleiser lists disinterested motive, and this is worth stopping for. Disinterested does not mean bored. It means having nothing to gain — impartial, with no stake in the outcome. A disinterested observer is a compliment; it says the person has no reason to lie. If you mean bored, the word is uninterested. The distinction is quietly disappearing, and holding onto it still marks careful writing.

Needs a trim

Some of Kleiser's phrases are the right idea in Edwardian dress. Keep the structure, change the adjective.

Studious reserve becomes deliberate restraint. Measured cadence becomes a measured tone. Guarded statement survives unchanged and is extremely useful for describing a careful answer. Sweeping assertion and hasty generalization are both still current and both excellent for pushing back on an argument without insulting anyone. Trite remark and hackneyed statement still work, though tired or clichéd will do the same job with less starch.

Dead, and deservedly so

Now the pleasure. Kleiser also offers bombastic prating, prolegomenous babbler, concatenated pedantries and hortatory moonshine. There is innocuous desuetude, which means harmless disuse and is itself a phrase in harmless disuse. There is petticoat diplomacy. Nothing on that list belongs in a modern email, but they are a useful reminder of what happens to language that is chosen to impress rather than to say something.

Kleiser knew the danger. He warns readers not to study fine phrases in order to insert them consciously into speech, because doing so leads straight to stiltedness. The man who compiled fifteen thousand phrases spent his introduction telling you not to show off with them.

Upgrade list

The main point → the salient point. Already decided → a foregone conclusion. Reasons it wasn't so bad → mitigating circumstances. Everyone assumed → a tacit assumption. A good argument → a cogent argument. A fair question → a pertinent question. Done badly and without care → perfunctory. A quick look → a cursory glance. A careful answer → a guarded statement. You're generalizing → that's a sweeping assertion. Neutral → disinterested.

Three cautions

First, one or two of these per email is plenty; a message built entirely from them reads as pompous. Second, mitigating and extenuating are near-synonyms, but extenuating is heavier and belongs in formal or legal contexts. Third, watch the register of the whole sentence — that's a sweeping assertion lands well in a meeting; your assertion is unduly sweeping sounds like 1917.

Try this. Take the last piece of work you reviewed and describe it in two sentences using salient, and either perfunctory or cursory. Precision here is kinder than vagueness: it tells the person exactly what to fix.
