The 80-Day Itinerary That Started It All

150 years before the gap year, one Englishman bet his fortune he could lap the planet in under three months. Pack light; the clock is ticking.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Jules Verne, Around the World in Eighty Days◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

Picture the ultimate itinerary: London to Suez, across to Bombay, over the rooftops of India by rail (mind the missing section of track), on to Hong Kong, Yokohama, San Francisco, and a white-knuckle dash across the American plains — all in eighty days, in 1872, with no plane in sight. This is the original round-the-world trip, and Jules Verne planned it with the loving precision of a man who genuinely adored a railway timetable.

Our traveler is Phileas Fogg, a London gentleman so punctual you could set Greenwich by him. When his club friends scoff that the newly linked railways and steamships have "shrunk" the world, Fogg does what any reasonable person would: he bets half his fortune that he can circle the globe in eighty days, and leaves that same evening. With him goes Passepartout, a French valet who signed up for a quiet life and got the opposite — our comic relief and, handily, the reader's stand-in, gawping at everything Fogg refuses to notice.

What makes it work

As a travel narrative, the engine of the book is beautifully simple: the deadline. Every leg is a small logistics puzzle — a delayed steamer, a monsoon, an elephant hired at an eye-watering price, a bridge that may or may not hold. Verne turns a railway timetable into a page-turner. And the ending delivers one of literature's most satisfying travel hacks: without spoiling it, let's just say that anyone who has flown east across the international date line knows the delicious feeling of getting a whole day back for free. Fogg's entire wager turns on it.

There is romance, too — Aouda, an Indian widow whom Fogg rescues along the way — and a detective, Fix, doggedly shadowing our hero in the belief that he is a fleeing bank robber. The plot runs like the trains: on time.

Where the guidebook gets it wrong

And yet — and every honest travel writer must file this complaint — Fogg is the worst kind of tourist. He crosses the most dazzling places on earth with his nose in a card game, treating India, China, and Japan as squares on a board rather than countries with insides. Verne's "exotic" East is scenery painted from a Paris armchair (he never made the trip), and it shows: the local color is broad, the cultures are backdrops, and the colonial gaze of the era sits heavily on every port.

Aouda, sadly, gets the Daisy treatment — more plot device than person, rescued and betrothed with barely a line of her own. And if you come for lush description, you will leave hungry. This is a travelogue written by a stopwatch.

The verdict

So is it worth the ticket? Absolutely — as long as you know what you are booking. Read it not for the destinations but for the going: the momentum, the clockwork plotting, and the giddy Victorian optimism that the whole planet had suddenly become reachable. It is less a guide to the world than a monument to the moment humans first felt it grow small. Just do yourself a favor Fogg never managed: look out the window.
