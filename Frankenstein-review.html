Frankenstein: A Peer Review, 200 Years Late

Two hundred years on, Mary Shelley's lab notebook remains the most cited cautionary tale in science. We reassess the methods — and the appalling lack of ethical oversight.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Mary Shelley, Frankenstein◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

In the summer of 1816, a nineteen-year-old set a challenge in motion that the scientific community has yet to fully answer: if you can create life, are you then responsible for it? Mary Shelley's "Frankenstein" is routinely miscited — the creature has no name; "Frankenstein" is the scientist — but its central finding has only grown more relevant. This is not a horror story about a monster. It is a case study in what happens when a brilliant researcher ships a product with no safety review and no plan for aftercare.

Background

Victor Frankenstein, an ambitious young natural philosopher, becomes obsessed with the "principle of life." Working alone, in secret, and increasingly sleep-deprived — a methodology this journal cannot endorse — he assembles a living being from salvaged parts and animates it. The exact protocol is, conveniently, never disclosed: Shelley infuses a spark and moves on, sparing us the wiring diagram. (Reproducibility: poor.)

Results

The experiment succeeds, which is precisely the problem. Confronted with the living result of his own work, Frankenstein does the one thing no responsible principal investigator ever should: he flees the lab in disgust and abandons the subject entirely. The creature — abandoned, articulate, and heartbreakingly reasonable — teaches itself to speak and read, asks only to be treated with kindness, and turns to violence solely after every human being recoils from it. The horror, Shelley argues, is not the creation. It is the neglect.

Discussion

Here the paper earns its two centuries of citations. Strip away the gothic weather and "Frankenstein" is an ethics seminar in disguise, and its questions land harder every year: who is accountable for what they build? Does creating something intelligent oblige you to care for it? Silicon Valley, currently assembling minds it does not fully understand and releasing them at midnight, might benefit from a careful re-read. Shelley got there first, and in a ballgown.

Limitations

Peer review, however, must note some flaws. The creature's education is implausibly rapid — it masters Milton and moral philosophy essentially by eavesdropping, a learning curve that strains belief. The narrative structure — a letter, containing a story, containing another story — is the literary equivalent of nested parentheses, and just as easy to get lost in. Victor himself is an exhausting narrator: he faints, he laments, he takes very little responsibility, and he never once considers simply talking to the being he made. By modern standards, the melodrama runs hot.

Conclusion

Nonetheless, the work stands. "Frankenstein" founded science fiction and, more importantly, gave the field its conscience — the nagging reminder that the question is never only "can we?" but "and then what?" Two hundred years later, no one has bettered it. Recommended reading, with a single condition: this time, do not abandon what you start.
