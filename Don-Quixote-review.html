The Man Who Read Too Much

He read adventure stories until he believed them, then went looking for giants. Four hundred years on, the first modern novel is still the funniest and saddest book about belief.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Miguel de Cervantes, Don Quixote◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

A man in his fifties sits up all night reading adventure stories. He sells land to buy more of them. He stops sleeping. And one morning he takes down a suit of rusted armor from the wall, gives his exhausted farm horse a heroic name, and rides out of his village to fix the world.

That is the whole engine of the book. Miguel de Cervantes published the first part in 1605: a country gentleman from La Mancha, in central Spain, has read so many tales of knights that he decides to become one. He renames himself Don Quixote, recruits a neighbor named Sancho Panza as his squire by promising him an island to govern, and sets off down the road looking for wrongs to right. Almost everyone he meets is baffled.

The scene everyone knows

Thirty pages in comes the image that outlived the book. Don Quixote sees windmills on the plain, decides they are giants with long waving arms, and charges. The lance goes into a sail, horse and rider are lifted and thrown, and Sancho arrives to point out, again, that they were windmills. His master explains that an evil magician turned the giants into windmills at the last moment to rob him of the glory.

That is why the scene endures. It is not simply about a fool. It is about the machinery a person builds to keep a belief alive after the evidence has landed on top of him. We now say tilting at windmills for attacking imaginary enemies, and four hundred years later we still need the phrase.

The other half

Sancho makes the book work. He is practical, hungry, greedy for his island, and completely aware that inns are inns and sheep are sheep. Yet he follows anyway. What begins as a straight double act — the dreamer and the realist — slowly turns into something stranger: the more the world mocks his master, the more fiercely Sancho defends him, and by the end it is no longer clear which of the two has changed the other more.

The joke is set up so that we laugh, and then arranged so that we are ashamed of laughing.

The trick in Part Two

Ten years later Cervantes published a second part, and did something no novelist had done. The characters in Part Two have read Part One. Don Quixote meets people who know his adventures because they are in print, and some of them stage elaborate practical jokes to watch him perform. A duke and duchess take him in purely for entertainment.

The effect is not clever for its own sake. It changes the moral weather. In Part One we laugh at a man who mistakes the world; in Part Two we watch bored aristocrats manufacture humiliations for him, and the laughter curdles. Cervantes builds the joke, then turns and looks at the people enjoying it.

Where it asks too much

Honesty requires the warning: it is very long, and it does not move in a straight line. The first part in particular keeps stopping so that a minor character can tell an unrelated novella, and readers have been skipping those since the seventeenth century. Some of the physical comedy is repetitive — a great deal of falling off horses and being beaten with sticks. And the cruelty is real; modern readers often find Part Two harder to enjoy than the slapstick of Part One, which may be the point but is still uncomfortable.

Why it lasts

Because the ending is not a punchline. Don Quixote comes home, recovers his sanity, renounces the books that ruined him, and dies quietly — and it is Sancho, the realist, who stands at the bedside begging him to get up and ride out again, insisting there are still giants. Cervantes spends nine hundred pages making us laugh at a man for believing in something, and then makes us grieve when he stops. Take the long road. Skip the novellas if you must.
