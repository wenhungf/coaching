The First Travel Influencer

He came home after twenty-four years with a story nobody believed, dictated it to a novelist in a prison cell, and changed how Europe saw the world. But did he ever actually go?

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Marco Polo, The Travels◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

The book that invented travel writing was dictated in a prison cell, by a merchant who had been away for twenty-four years, to a cellmate who wrote romance novels for a living. Seven hundred years later, we are still arguing about how much of it is true.

The subject: Marco Polo, a Venetian trader who left home at seventeen with his father and uncle, crossed Persia and the mountains of Central Asia, and arrived at the court of Kublai Khan, the Mongol emperor who then ruled China. He stayed roughly seventeen years, apparently in the Khan's service, and came home with a description of Asia so far beyond European experience that his neighbors laughed at him.

The pitch

What he was selling, essentially, was scale. Europe in 1300 was a place of small towns and wooden bridges. Polo described cities of a million people, canals carrying grain across an empire, and a postal system of relay stations where riders changed horses and covered distances no European courier could match.

Three things astonished his readers most. Paper money — the idea that a state could print marks on mulberry bark and have everyone accept it as wealth. Black stones that burned, hotter and longer than wood, dug out of the ground: coal, which Europe had barely begun to use. And a mail network so fast it seemed like magic. He was not describing a foreign country. He was describing the future.

Fact-check

Now the difficulty. Polo spent seventeen years in China and never mentions tea. He never mentions chopsticks. He does not describe foot-binding, or Chinese writing, and he says nothing whatever about the Great Wall. His name has not been found in any Mongol record, despite his claim to have governed a city and served as an envoy.

Skeptics conclude that he never went, and assembled the book from Persian merchants' accounts picked up along the trade routes. Defenders answer that he moved in a Mongol court that used Persian, not Chinese, and among people who did not drink tea; that the Wall as we picture it was largely built later; and that his details on salt taxes, currency, and provincial administration are far too specific for a man inventing things. The honest position is somewhere in the middle, and unresolved.

The ghostwriter problem

One fact deserves more attention than it usually gets. Polo did not write the book. He told it, in a Genoese prison, to a fellow prisoner named Rustichello of Pisa — a professional author of chivalric romances, the popular knights-and-quests fiction of the day. Read the text with that in mind and the tone makes sense: the battles are staged, the kings are magnificent, and the marvels arrive on cue. A reporter and a novelist collaborated, and only one of them had been there.

Where it wears badly

As a reading experience it can be a slog. Long stretches read like an inventory — this province produces ginger, that one produces silk, the people are idolaters and subject to the Great Khan — repeated for chapter after chapter. Polo himself barely appears; he almost never tells us what he felt. And hearsay sits beside eyewitness reporting with no signal to separate them, which is precisely what makes the fact-checking so hard.

The verdict

His contemporaries nicknamed the book Il Milione — the million, meaning a million lies. Asked on his deathbed to take back the exaggerations, Polo is said to have replied that he had not told the half of what he saw. Read it as the first great act of travel reporting, dictated by a man with an extraordinary memory and edited by one with an extraordinary imagination. Somewhere in there is Asia in 1290, and nothing else in Europe came close for two hundred years.
