A Case of Fatal Fright

No wound, a face frozen in terror, and the footprints of a giant dog. We review the case notes of the most famous diagnosis in English fiction.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Arthur Conan Doyle, The Hound of the Baskervilles◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

A country doctor arrives at a consulting room in London and presents an unusual case. His patient is dead. The body showed no wound, the face was fixed in terror, and beside the corpse in the mud were the footprints of a gigantic hound.

Arthur Conan Doyle published this in 1902, and it remains the best-constructed of the Sherlock Holmes stories. Sir Charles Baskerville has died on the moor of Devon, and his family has for two centuries been said to be hunted by a supernatural dog. The heir, arriving from Canada, may be next. Holmes sends Doctor Watson to Devon to observe, and stays behind in London — or appears to.

History and presentation

The case notes are excellent because Watson is a doctor, and Doyle wrote him that way deliberately. Watson records what he sees in order, distinguishes what he witnessed from what he was told, and does not speculate beyond the evidence. He is, in effect, taking a patient history: onset, circumstances, prior episodes in the family, and the reports of neighbors, all set down before any conclusion is offered.

The setting does the rest. The moor is a wide, wet, treeless upland with a bog that swallows animals whole, and Doyle uses it as a symptom rather than scenery: sound carries strangely, lights appear where nobody should be, and a man with a lantern is visible for miles. Half the mystery is a landscape problem.

Differential diagnosis

Holmes's method is the one taught in medical schools, arrived at from the other direction. Doyle studied medicine in Edinburgh under Joseph Bell, a surgeon famous for telling patients their occupation and recent travels before they had finished sitting down. Bell was demonstrating that careful observation of small signs yields large conclusions, and his student turned him into a detective.

So the book proceeds as a differential: list every condition that fits the presentation, then eliminate. A family curse fits the fear but not the footprints. A wild animal fits the prints but not the timing. Each hypothesis is tested against a fact that will not bend, and the one that survives is the answer.

List everything that fits the evidence. Eliminate what cannot. What remains is your answer.

Complications

Some findings are less impressive on review. Holmes is absent for a large stretch of the middle, and while his reappearance is one of the great moments in detective fiction, the reader spends many chapters with a narrator who is honest, thorough and consistently one step behind.

The solution also relies on information the reader could not have had: a portrait, a document, a resemblance nobody mentions until Holmes points at it. By the standards of the fair-play mystery that came later, this is a violation. Doyle was not writing a puzzle to be beaten; he was writing a performance to be admired, and the difference matters if you like to guess.

Finally, the ending is quick. After two hundred pages of atmosphere, the explanation is delivered briskly in a drawing room, and the terror that made the book famous evaporates in a paragraph of plain motive.

Conclusion

Still the finest entry point to Holmes, and the only one where the landscape is as frightening as the crime. Read it for the method: a way of looking at a mess of details and asking, patiently, which of these things cannot be explained by the obvious answer. Doyle learned that from a doctor. It works on more than crimes.
