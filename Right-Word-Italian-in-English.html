One Panino, Two Panini

The plural trap, the language of music, and why a crescendo is the climb rather than the summit — plus the espresso that has no x.

InkQuest Reading Room◆The Right Word · Loanwords◆Level B1–C1◆~5 min read

If you have ever ordered a panini, you have made the single most common Italian mistake in English. The word is already plural. One of them is a panino — and once you notice this pattern, you will find it everywhere.

Italian came into English along two roads: music, almost entirely, from the seventeenth century onward; and food, mostly in the twentieth, carried by emigration. A third, smaller road brought the vocabulary of art and banking. Each road left its own accent.

The plural trap

Italian forms plurals by changing the final vowel, not by adding -s. English borrowed many words already in the plural, then treated them as singular. The result is a set of words almost everyone uses incorrectly, and a few worth knowing precisely.

Panini is plural; panino is one. Biscotti is plural; biscotto is one. Ravioli, spaghetti, linguine and gnocchi are all plural — which is why careful writers say the spaghetti are in Italian but happily say the spaghetti is in English, because English has absorbed it as a mass noun.

Three more where the plural still matters. Graffiti is plural, and a single mark is a graffito. Paparazzi is plural; one photographer is a paparazzo. Confetti was originally plural too. Nobody will correct you in a cafe, but in writing about art or media, the singular forms are worth having.

The language of music is Italian

Nearly every instruction on a page of Western sheet music is Italian, and the words have all escaped into ordinary English.

Tempo is the speed of a piece — and now the speed of anything, including a negotiation. Allegro is fast and cheerful; adagio is slow. Crescendo means growing louder, and here is the point most people miss: a crescendo is the increase, not the peak. Rising to a crescendo is, strictly, a mistake; the crescendo is the rise itself. Diminuendo is the opposite. Staccato notes are short and detached, which is why we speak of staccato speech. Legato is smooth and connected. A solo is played alone; a duet is two; a virtuoso is a performer of extraordinary technical skill. A maestro is a master, usually a conductor. And an aria is a solo song in an opera.

A crescendo is the climb, not the summit.

Food, art and everything else

Food gave English al dente (cooked so it still has bite), antipasto (before the meal), espresso (note: not expresso), and cappuccino (named for the color of a Capuchin friar's robe). Al fresco means outdoors, though amusingly it does not mean that in modern Italian.

Art and life gave chiaroscuro, the strong contrast of light and dark in a painting; fresco, painting onto wet plaster; and studio, originally a place of study. Then the social words: a fiasco is a total failure, from the Italian for flask; sprezzatura is the art of making difficult things look effortless; and dolce far niente is the sweetness of doing nothing, which has no English equivalent and probably should.

A worked example

Weak: The presentation started slow and then got much faster and louder at the end, and it was kind of a disaster.

Precise: The presentation opened adagio — three slides of background nobody needed — then built to a genuine crescendo in the final section, where the numbers finally appeared. The delivery was staccato throughout, which suited the material. Question time, unfortunately, was a fiasco.

Upgrade list

Speed of the thing → tempo. Getting louder and bigger → a crescendo. Short and choppy → staccato. Smooth and flowing → legato. Really good at it → a virtuoso. Cooked just right, still firm → al dente. Outside → al fresco. Total disaster → a fiasco. Making it look easy → sprezzatura. Strong light and shadow → chiaroscuro.

Three things to get right

First, espresso has no x. Second, a latte in Italy is a glass of milk; the drink is a caffe latte, and ordering by the short form abroad may get you exactly what you asked for. Third, pronounce the double consonants: in Italian words English has kept, the doubled letter is held slightly longer, and cappuccino with a single soft c sounds noticeably wrong to anyone who knows.

Try this. Describe something you watched recently — a match, a meeting, a concert — using tempo, crescendo and one food word used figuratively. If the ending disappointed you, the word you want is fiasco.
