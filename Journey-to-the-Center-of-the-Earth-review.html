Expedition Report: Down the Volcano

In 1864 Jules Verne sent three men down an Icelandic crater to look for the middle of the Earth. We assess the route, the kit, and the one team member who truly earned his pay.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Jules Verne, Journey to the Center of the Earth◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

Every expedition begins with a document. This one begins with a scrap of parchment covered in runes, tumbling out of a secondhand book in a Hamburg study, and a professor who will not eat or sleep until he has cracked the code.

Decoded, the note is an invitation. Arne Saknussemm, a sixteenth-century Icelandic scholar, claims he found a road to the center of the Earth and left directions for anyone reckless enough to follow. Professor Otto Lidenbrock packs that same afternoon. His nephew Axel, who would much rather stay home and marry his sweetheart, is not consulted. In Reykjavik the two hire a local guide named Hans, and in late June they climb into the crater of an extinct volcano, Snæfellsjökull, and start walking downward.

The route

The descent is the finest thing in the book. The party follows old lava tubes — the long hollow pipes left behind when molten rock drains away — deeper and deeper into cold basalt. They take a wrong turn at a fork and nearly die of thirst; Hans saves them by listening to a wall, swinging a pickaxe at it, and releasing an underground stream, which the men name the Hansbach and then follow downward like a handrail for weeks.

Far below, the tunnel opens onto something nobody has prepared for: a sea. It has its own beach, its own storms, and a pale electric sky. Mushrooms grow to the height of trees. Out on the water, from the safety of a raft, the men watch two creatures that should have been extinct for a hundred million years tear each other apart.

The kit list

Verne, ever the engineer, itemizes everything: rope ladders, pickaxes, six months of dried food, water flasks, and two Ruhmkorff lamps — real 1860s battery-powered lights, which let the party keep the tunnels lit without carrying an open flame into rock that might hold gas. The provisioning is meticulous. The one thing they almost die of is water, which is precisely the mistake real expeditions still make.

Hans is the finest character Verne ever wrote, and he barely says a word.

Personnel

Axel narrates, and he is a coward — anxious, homesick, prone to fainting at the worst moment. That is exactly why he works: he asks the questions we would ask and panics on our behalf. Lidenbrock is a force of nature with no patience and no doubt. But the expedition survives because of Hans, who never raises his voice, never hurries, repairs whatever breaks, and once a week, several kilometers beneath Iceland, calmly asks to be paid his wages.

Where the science collapses

Now, the geology. It does not hold up. Dig down and the temperature climbs fast; long before you reach any of Verne's caverns you would meet rock hot enough to flow. Verne knew this, has Axel raise the objection, and then lets Lidenbrock brush it aside — a magnificent piece of authorial nerve. The way out is worse: a volcanic eruption does the job, and the physics of that does not bear thinking about. The characters, too, are functions rather than people: the doubter, the driver, the fixer.

The verdict

None of which matters much. Read it for the going down — the narrowing walls, the failing light, the moment a tunnel opens onto an impossible shore. Verne built the template that every descent story has followed since, and he built it out of rope, lamplight, and nerve. Just take his one real lesson with you: carry more water than you think you need.
