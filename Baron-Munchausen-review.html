We Rate These Claims: False

He rode a cannonball, visited the moon twice, and escaped a swamp by pulling his own hair. We examined every claim in the book. The results are consistent.

Reviewed for InkQuest Reading Room◆Rudolf Erich Raspe, Baron Munchausen◆Level B1–C1◆~4 min read

The claim before us is that our subject once escaped a swamp by seizing his own hair and pulling himself out, horse included. We have examined this claim. It does not hold.

Some background. The book collects the tall tales of Baron Munchausen, a German nobleman who really existed — Hieronymus von Münchhausen fought for Russia against the Turks and, back home, entertained dinner guests with wildly exaggerated war stories. In 1785 a writer named Rudolf Erich Raspe published those stories, anonymously, in English, without asking. The real baron was appalled. He spent the rest of his life as a punchline.

Claim: he rode a cannonball

The Baron states that, wanting to inspect an enemy fortress, he leapt onto a cannonball as it was fired and rode it toward the walls. Mid-flight he reconsidered, spotted an incoming cannonball travelling the other way, jumped across, and returned to his own lines.

Rating: false. A cannonball leaves the barrel at several hundred meters per second. Our findings suggest the Baron would not have survived the mounting, and we note that the second half of the story requires two cannonballs to pass within arm's reach at combined speeds no rider could act upon. We rate the anecdote physically impossible and narratively perfect.

Claim: he visited the moon

Twice, in fact. On the first occasion he climbed a bean plant that had grown up to it. On the second he was carried there by a storm while at sea. He reports that the inhabitants are enormous, ride three-headed birds, and remove their heads to be cleaned.

Rating: false, though we award credit for consistency: the Baron never reports being surprised. This is the technical secret of the whole book. He describes the impossible in the flat tone of a man describing a delayed train, and the calm is what makes the reader laugh.

He never reports being surprised. That is the entire trick.

Claim: he pulled himself out of a swamp by his own hair

Rating: false, and instructively so. You cannot lift yourself by pulling upward on your own body; there is nothing external to push against. English has a phrase for exactly this fallacy — to pull yourself up by your own bootstraps, meaning to succeed with no help from anyone — and it began life as a joke about something obviously impossible. It is now used, without irony, as career advice.

Assessment of the source

The Baron is the least reliable narrator in European literature, and the book knows it. Every episode is delivered to an audience after dinner, and the pleasure is not in believing him but in watching him refuse to blink. He is never caught out, never admits doubt, and treats every listener's silence as agreement.

The weaknesses are real. There is no plot whatever — the book is a heap of episodes in no particular order, and it stops rather than ends. Later editions were padded by other hands, so quality swings wildly from chapter to chapter. Read more than twenty pages at a sitting and the formula shows: outrageous situation, absurd solution, no consequences, next.

Our finding

Everything the Baron says is false. Almost everything he says is worth reading. The book gave psychiatry a term for people who invent illnesses for attention, and it gave the rest of us a working portrait of the confident liar — the man who wins the room not with evidence but with an absolute refusal to be embarrassed. Two hundred and forty years later, we are still fact-checking him, and he is still not sorry.
